# Auto-Suggestion

*Notes on Chapter 4 of Napoleon Hill's* Think and Grow Rich *— "The Third Step Toward Riches."*

---

## The definition

**Auto-suggestion is self-suggestion.** Hill uses the term for every self-administered stimulus that reaches the mind through the five senses.

Its job in his system is narrow and specific: it is **the agency of communication** between the part of the mind where conscious thought takes place and the part that serves as the seat of action — the subconscious.

The mechanism runs through the thoughts a person *permits to remain* in the conscious mind. Note the verb. Hill is not describing thoughts that arrive; he is describing thoughts that are allowed to stay.

Whether those thoughts are negative or positive is, to the mechanism, **immaterial**. Auto-suggestion carries whatever it is handed.

---

## The gate

Hill's model of how it works:

1. Sense impressions arrive through the five senses.
2. **They are stopped by the conscious thinking mind.**
3. There they are either passed on to the subconscious or rejected.

In his phrase, the conscious faculty serves as **"an outer guard to the approach of the subconscious."**

From which he draws the claim the whole chapter rests on: nature has so built a person that he has **absolute control** over the material that reaches his subconscious mind through the five senses.

And immediately the honest qualification, which is the more interesting half — this is not to say a person *exercises* that control. In the great majority of instances he does not. Hill's account of why so many go through life in poverty is not that the gate is locked against them but that it is standing unattended.

## The garden

The image Hill returns to: the subconscious as **a fertile garden spot, in which weeds will grow in abundance if the seeds of more desirable crops are not sown there.**

Fertility is not the variable. The ground will produce either way. Auto-suggestion is the agency through which a person either deliberately feeds the subconscious on thoughts of a creative nature, or **by neglect** permits thoughts of a destructive nature to find their way into that rich garden.

There is no third condition where nothing grows.

---

## Where it sits in the book

Chapter 4 is not freestanding. It is the machinery that makes two other chapters operate:

- **Back to Chapter 2 (Desire).** The last of the six steps instructed the reader to read aloud, twice daily, the written statement of his desire for money — and to **see and feel himself already in possession** of it. Hill's point here is that this instruction *was* auto-suggestion; the reader was applying the principle before it had been named. He asks the reader to go back and re-read the six steps before going further.
- **Forward to Organized Planning.** The four instructions for forming a Master Mind group are the same principle applied with other people in the room.

Set the three side by side and the architecture shows: desire supplies the content, auto-suggestion supplies the delivery, the Master Mind supplies the reinforcement.

---

## The one thing that makes it work

This is the load-bearing paragraph of the chapter, and Hill says outright that he repeats it in practically every chapter because failing to grasp it is *the main reason* most people who try the principle get nothing from it.

> **The mere reading of the words is of no consequence unless you mix emotion, or feeling, with your words.**

His example is **Émile Coué's** famous formula — *"Day by day, in every way, I am getting better and better."* Repeat it a million times without mixing emotion and faith into it, Hill says, and you will experience no desirable results.

The rule stated plainly:

> **Your subconscious mind recognizes and acts upon only thoughts which have been well mixed with emotion or feeling.**
>
> **Plain, unemotional words do not influence the subconscious mind.**

So the practice is not recitation. Recitation with the feeling absent is the failure mode, and it is the *common* failure mode — the person doing it believes they are following the instructions exactly, because in every visible respect they are.

---

## The price

Hill anticipates the reader who tries it once, feels nothing, and concludes it doesn't work. Do not be discouraged if you cannot control and direct your emotions the first time.

Then the terms:

> **There is no such possibility as something for nothing.** The ability to reach and influence your subconscious mind has its price, and you must pay that price. You cannot cheat, even if you desire to do so.
>
> The price is **everlasting persistence.**

Two things worth noticing about how he frames that. First, it is a *price*, not a talent — available to anyone, denied to no one by circumstance. Second, he closes with *you, and you alone* — the responsibility is not shareable, which is the same move that makes the chapter bracing and makes its edge sharp.

---

## Reading it honestly

Some of the chapter is method and some is metaphysics, and they are worth separating before this gets taught.

- **The method holds up well.** Written statement, spoken aloud, twice daily, with the outcome vividly imagined and genuinely felt, sustained over a long period. Strip the vocabulary and that is attention management and rehearsal — what a person repeatedly attends to with feeling does shape what they notice, what they expect, and what they attempt.
- **"Thoughts picked up from the ether"** is Hill's one carve-out to his own model, and it is 1937 metaphysics rather than anything demonstrable. It is a passing clause; nothing in the practical instruction depends on it.
- **The conscious mind as an "outer guard" with "absolute control"** is a clean model, not a finding. Anyone who has lain awake with a thought they did not choose knows the gate is not that tight. Hill's practical claim — that you have *far more* say over what you dwell on than you exercise — survives the overstatement.
- **"Which explains why so many people go through life in poverty"** is the line to handle with care. As a claim about attention and habit it is partly true. As a total explanation of poverty it is false, and it lands hard on people whose circumstances were authored by someone other than themselves. See the caution below.

## The connection to Chapter 3

The U.S. Steel story is the previous chapter's illustration ([notes here](us-steel-formation-history.md)), and it doubles as this chapter's.

Charles M. Schwab did not stand up at the University Club and read a prepared valuation. He had carried the vision of a consolidated steel industry long enough and felt it strongly enough that ninety unscripted minutes of it poured out under the mildest possible prompting — at a dinner where he had been advised to say nothing of substance.

That is Hill's point about emotionalized thought, demonstrated rather than asserted. The idea had been fed and watered until it was the thing most present in the man. The six sheets of copperplate figures were what made it credible; the feeling behind it was what made it *move* a room of eighty financiers who had come expecting polite vaporings.

Desire supplied the content. Auto-suggestion had done the work long before the night it paid.

## For the faith angle

The chapter is closer to the ministry's territory than its vocabulary suggests, and further from it in one specific place.

Close, in these:

- **Guarding the gate.** Hill's outer guard is the practice of watching what you let take up residence — the thing already commanded as guarding the heart, and as taking thoughts captive. He arrives at it from mechanics; the conclusion is the same.
- **Spoken aloud, twice daily, in a spirit of absolute faith.** Hill has independently reconstructed a discipline of confession and daily devotion, including the insistence that it be *aloud* and *repeated* rather than merely believed in the abstract.
- **The garden.** Weeds need no planting. Anyone who has taught on the soils will recognize the ground Hill is standing on.
- **"You cannot cheat, even if you desire to do so."** A man will reap what he sows, stated as a law rather than a threat.
- **Feeling is not optional.** The warning against a million recitations without emotion is the same warning against honoring with the lips while the heart is far off. Hill found the identical failure mode and gave it a mechanical explanation.

Where it parts company, and this is worth saying plainly if it's taught:

- **The direction of the transaction is reversed.** Hill's practice is aimed at conditioning oneself. Prayer is addressed to Someone. The forms rub close enough that the difference can be lost by accident, and a class will lose it if nobody names it.
- **"You, and you alone."** That is the chapter's final word and its cleanest break. Faith that has to be self-generated, self-sustained, and self-financed is a heavier load than the one being offered.
- **The poverty claim** cuts against people the ministry is likely serving. Take Hill's method — the attention, the repetition, the persistence — without taking his verdict on why people are poor.

Used with those distinctions stated out loud, the chapter is genuinely useful: it is a serious, practical answer to *how do you get a thing you believe out of your head and into your habits*, and most people never get a straight answer to that question.

---

## The practice, in short

1. **Write the statement down.** Definite, specific, in words.
2. **Read it aloud twice daily** — morning and night.
3. **See and feel yourself already in possession** of the thing. Feeling is the active ingredient, not the decoration.
4. **Expect the feeling to be hard to summon at first.** That is not failure; it is the beginning of the price.
5. **Keep at it past the point where it feels productive.** Everlasting persistence is the stated cost, and Hill says it cannot be negotiated down.
