# The Idea That Built U.S. Steel

*Background notes for the United States Steel story told at the close of Chapter 3 ("Faith") of Napoleon Hill's* Think and Grow Rich.

---

## What the chapter is actually claiming

Hill uses the formation of the United States Steel Corporation as his worked example of a single principle: **an idea, held with faith and stated with definiteness, is itself the asset.** Everything else — the mills, the ore boats, the coke ovens, the $1.4 billion of paper — followed a speech that one man gave at a dinner and could not have been paid for at any price beforehand.

The passage you were listening to is the hinge into that story. Hill's setup runs roughly: it will be both interesting and profitable to analyze an event that gives an understanding of the method by which industrialists and businessmen accumulate great fortunes by *giving before they try to get*. The illustration dates back to 1900, when the United States Steel Corporation was being formed.

Hill then reprints an article by **John Lowell** from the *New York World-Telegram*, which is where most of the narrative detail below comes from in the book.

---

## The cast

| Person | Role in 1900–01 |
|---|---|
| **Charles M. Schwab** | 38-year-old president of Carnegie Steel Company. Rose from stake-driver to the top of the largest steel maker in the country. |
| **Andrew Carnegie** | Owner of Carnegie Steel; the man who had to be persuaded to sell. |
| **J. Pierpont Morgan** | The banker with the capital and the appetite for consolidation. |
| **J. Edward Simmons & Charles Stewart Smith** | New York bankers who hosted the dinner, in return for Schwab's hospitality on a recent visit to Pittsburgh. |
| **John W. "Bet-a-Million" Gates** | Steel and wire man, present at the all-night negotiation. |
| **Robert Bacon** | Morgan partner, present at the same conference. |
| **Elbert H. Gary** | Morgan's lawyer; became chairman of the new corporation. |

---

## Timeline

- **December 12, 1900** — Dinner at the University Club on Fifth Avenue, New York. About eighty of the country's financiers and industrialists attend, gathered to introduce Schwab to eastern banking society. Carnegie helped arrange it, appeared at the reception, and left before the speech.
- **That same evening** — Schwab speaks. Morgan, seated beside him, takes him aside afterward for a private conversation in a corner of the room.
- **January 1901** — An all-night conference at Morgan's home on Madison Avenue: Morgan, Bacon, Gates, and Schwab. Schwab works out on paper the valuations of the companies to be merged.
- **Late January 1901** — Schwab takes the question to Carnegie. Carnegie thinks it over and hands Schwab a slip of paper with his price written on it.
- **Early February 1901** — Morgan accepts. Terms concluded.
- **February 25, 1901** — United States Steel Corporation is incorporated in New Jersey.
- **April 1, 1901** — The corporation begins operations.

---

## The dinner

Not half a dozen of the eighty guests understood what they were about to witness. The room contained the Vanderbilt and Harriman railroad interests, bankers including Stillman, Belmont, and Schiff, and H. H. Rogers of Standard Oil. Schwab was the young man from out of the West being shown to the East.

He had not planned to make history. He was expected to say a few gracious words.

## The speech

Schwab spoke without notes. Accounts of the length differ — contemporary reporting puts it near a half hour, while the retelling in *Think and Grow Rich* runs it to ninety minutes — but no account disputes what he did with the time.

Instead of thanking his hosts and sitting down, he laid out an argument: the American steel industry, as then organized, was wasteful. A dozen companies were each trying to make everything, competing themselves into inefficiency. The future belonged to a single consolidated enterprise in which each plant specialized in one product, costs were driven relentlessly downward, and the surplus was pushed into foreign markets. He described, in effect, a supertrust running from raw ore to finished product — and he described its earning power.

Morgan, by the standard account, sat through it so absorbed that he let his cigar go out. When it was over he drew Schwab aside and kept him in the corner for the better part of an hour.

**The point Hill draws out:** Schwab did not ask for anything. He gave a vision away, in full, to a room that had no obligation to him. The transaction came looking for him afterward.

## The all-night conference

Morgan summoned Schwab to Madison Avenue. Through a night of talk, Schwab did the thing that separated him from every other enthusiast in the room: he put numbers on it. Which companies. What each was worth. What the combination would earn. Morgan's own steel interests, Carnegie's, and a set of smaller steel, mining, and shipping concerns were fitted into a single structure.

The vision survived contact with arithmetic. That is why it became a corporation rather than an after-dinner story.

## The slip of paper

The obstacle was Carnegie, who did not obviously want to sell. Schwab put the question to him, reportedly in the course of a golf game, and Carnegie took a night to think.

The next morning he handed Schwab a slip of paper with a figure written on it — usually given as **$480 million**. Morgan looked at it and accepted without negotiating. The final consideration for the Carnegie interests came to about **$492 million** in bonds and stock of the new company; Carnegie's personal share was roughly **$226 million** (the figure often quoted precisely as $225,639,000), taken largely in 5% gold bonds.

The most-repeated coda: crossing the Atlantic some time later, Carnegie remarked to Morgan that he supposed he should have asked $100 million more, and Morgan answered that he would have got it if he had asked. The exchange is a fixture of the retellings — including Hill's — but treat it as anecdote rather than record.

## The numbers

| | |
|---|---|
| Authorized capitalization | ~$1.4 billion — the first billion-dollar corporation in history |
| Rough structure | ~$508M common stock, ~$500M+ preferred, ~$300–390M bonds (sources differ on the exact split) |
| Bureau of Corporations' later valuation of the underlying properties | ~$682 million |
| Share of U.S. steel output at formation | roughly two-thirds |

The gap between $682 million of property and $1.4 billion of paper was denounced at the time as **watered stock** — well over half a billion dollars of capitalization with no tangible asset behind it.

Hill's answer to that charge is the whole reason the story is in a chapter called Faith. The "water," he argues, was not fraud; it was the market's valuation of *the idea itself* — the earning power that consolidation would create and that had existed nowhere on earth before Schwab described it out loud at a dinner. The corporation subsequently earned the difference. The intangible was the largest single asset on the books, and it had been created by a man thinking.

---

## Hill's principle, stated plainly

1. **The asset was an idea.** Steel mills existed before December 12, 1900. The organizing thought did not.
2. **Faith is what carries an idea into speech.** Schwab presented a reorganization of an entire industry, uninvited, to men with the power to laugh at him.
3. **Definiteness converts belief into money.** The vision alone got him a conversation in the corner. The sheet of figures got him a corporation.
4. **Give before you try to get.** Schwab's speech asked for nothing. This is the specific mechanism Hill is pointing at in the passage you were listening to.
5. **The idea belonged to whoever would act on it.** Morgan supplied capital, Carnegie supplied the mills, but the thing being bought and sold had been assembled in Schwab's mind.

---

## Where the telling and the record diverge

Worth holding lightly if this gets taught or quoted:

- **Speech length.** Half an hour in contemporary accounts; ninety minutes in the *Think and Grow Rich* version.
- **The dollar figures.** Popular retellings vary in what they put on Carnegie's slip and what they give as the final price. The best-supported pair is $480 million written down, ~$492 million concluded.
- **The cigar.** Morgan forgetting to light or letting die his cigar is universally repeated and impossible to verify.
- **Sole authorship.** Consolidation was already in the air — Morgan had been assembling combinations for years, and Gates and others had been circling the same idea. Schwab's speech was the catalyst and the blueprint, not an idea with no antecedents. Hill's telling compresses this.
- **Carnegie's reluctance.** He had signaled willingness to sell at the right price before; the golf-course scene is dramatized in most versions.

None of this damages Hill's argument. It does mean the story should be told as *illustration*, not as citation.

---

## Afterward

- Schwab became the first president of the new corporation at Morgan's insistence — and lasted only into 1903, worn down by conflict with Gary, Frick, and the board. William Ellis Corey succeeded him.
- Schwab then took control of Bethlehem Steel and built it into the second largest producer in the country. His second act was arguably the better one.
- Carnegie, at 65 and the richest man in the world, turned to giving the money away — libraries, universities, endowments — the project that occupied the rest of his life.
- U.S. Steel remained the largest corporation in the world for decades, and never again held the share of the market it held on the day it opened.

---

## For the faith angle

The chapter is not really about steel. Hill is arguing that faith is an *applied* faculty: a thing believed, held with definiteness, spoken, and acted on before any evidence arrives that it will work. Schwab is his exhibit because the sequence is so clean and so datable — belief, declaration, cost paid up front, provision afterward.

The connections that preach:

- **The word goes out before the provision comes in.** Nothing in Schwab's circumstances on December 12 required him to speak. He spoke, and then the means appeared.
- **Faith without figures is enthusiasm.** The night at Morgan's house is the discipline half. Vision that refuses the work of specifics does not build anything.
- **Give before you try to get** is Hill's phrasing of a much older idea, and he knows it.

One honest caution if this is used in teaching: Hill's frame measures faith by what it accumulates. That is a narrower thing than the faith the ministry means, and the story is stronger when the difference is named out loud rather than blurred.
