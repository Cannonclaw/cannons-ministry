# The Idea That Built U.S. Steel

*Background notes for the United States Steel story told at the close of Chapter 3 ("Faith") of Napoleon Hill's* Think and Grow Rich.

---

## What the chapter is actually claiming

Hill uses the formation of the United States Steel Corporation as his worked example of a single principle: **an idea, held with faith and stated with definiteness, is itself the asset.** Everything else — the mills, the ore boats, the coke ovens, the $1.4 billion of paper — followed a speech that one man gave at a dinner and that could not have been bought at any price beforehand.

He sets it up as an analysis of how industrialists accumulate great fortunes **by giving before they try to get**, then reprints an article by **John Lowell** from the *New York World-Telegram* under the headline **"A Pretty After-Dinner Speech for a Billion Dollars."** Nearly all the narrative detail below is Lowell's.

## Hill's six steps

Before the article, Hill enumerates what he wants the reader to watch for. In his sequence, the United States Steel Corporation:

1. **Was born in the mind of Charles M. Schwab in the form of an idea**, created through his imagination.
2. **He mixed faith with the idea.**
3. **He formulated a plan** for transforming the idea into physical and financial reality.
4. **He put the plan into action** with his speech at the University Club.
5. **He followed through with persistence**, backed by firm decision, until it had been fully carried out.
6. **He prepared the way** with a burning desire for success.

The whole reason the story sits in Chapter 3 is step 2. Steps 1 and 3 through 6 are craft; step 2 is the one Hill says most people never take.

## The cast

| Person | Role in 1900–01 |
|---|---|
| **Charles M. Schwab** | 38-year-old president of Carnegie Steel; a career that "flowered along the banks of the Monongahela." In Hill's phrase, "practically unknown at the time — merely Andrew Carnegie's man Friday." |
| **Andrew Carnegie** | Owner of Carnegie Steel — "the Little Boss" — watching from Skibo Castle. His firm was a vertical trust owned and operated by 53 partners. |
| **J. Pierpont Morgan** | Seated at Schwab's right hand, intending to stay only briefly. "Steadfast in his belief in the divine right of money." |
| **J. Edward Simmons & Charles Stewart Smith** | The hosts, repaying Schwab's lavish hospitality on a recent visit to Pittsburgh. |
| **Robert Bacon** | Morgan's partner — "a scholar and a gentleman." |
| **John W. "Bet-a-Million" Gates** | Had formed American Steel and Wire and, with Morgan, Federal Steel. Urged a steel trust on Morgan, who "scorned him as a gambler and used him as a tool." Served as go-between. |
| **Bill & Jim Moore** | Chicago stock jobbers who had assembled a match trust and a cracker corporation, then American Tin Plate, Steel Hoop, Sheet Steel, and National Steel. Urged it and failed. |
| **Elbert H. Gary** | "The sanctimonious country lawyer" who wanted to foster it but "wasn't big enough to be impressive." Became chairman of the new corporation. |
| **James R. Keene** | The stock manipulator who placed the new shares with the public. |

## Timeline

- **December 12, 1900** — Dinner at the University Club on Fifth Avenue. About eighty of the nation's financial nobility, representing an estimated $5 billion of capital. Not half a dozen realize what they are about to witness.
- **That evening** — Schwab talks for ninety minutes. Morgan then leads him to a recessed window where, legs dangling from a high uncomfortable seat, they talk for an hour more.
- **The next day** — No mention of the affair finds its way into print.
- **About a week later** — Morgan, having digested the feast of reason, sends for Schwab. Schwab is coy: Carnegie would not like his company president flirting with the emperor of Wall Street.
- **A Sunday night** — After a proposed "coincidental" meeting at the Bellevue in Philadelphia is dropped (Morgan is ill at home), Schwab presents himself at Morgan's New York library carrying six sheets of copperplate figures in his own handwriting. Four men work through the night.
- **January 1901** — The golf game at St. Andrews in Westchester. Carnegie writes his price on a slip of paper.
- **Early February 1901** — Terms concluded.
- **February 25, 1901** — United States Steel Corporation incorporated in New Jersey.
- **April 1, 1901** — Operations begin.

## The dinner

The hosts had warned him. New York stuffed shirts would not be responsive to oratory; if he didn't want to bore the Stillmans and Harrimans and Vanderbilts, he should hold himself to fifteen or twenty minutes of **"polite vaporings"** and let it go at that. Morgan meant to grace the table with his presence and leave.

The guests ate through the usual seven or eight courses. Conversation was thin and restrained — few of the bankers had met Schwab, and none knew him well.

So little seemed to happen that the press ignored it entirely. It is unfortunate for history that no record of the speech was ever made. Schwab repeated parts of it later to a similar gathering of Chicago bankers, and later still gave his own version from the witness stand when the government sued to dissolve the steel trust.

## The speech

Probably homely. Somewhat ungrammatical — the niceties of language never bothered Schwab. Full of epigram, threaded with wit. And, on the $5 billion of estimated capital sitting in front of him, galvanic.

What he laid out was not flattery but a program:

- A world future for steel.
- Reorganization for efficiency; specialization of plants.
- Scrapping the unsuccessful mills, concentrating effort on the flourishing properties.
- Economies in ore traffic; economies in overhead and administration.
- Capturing foreign markets.

Then he turned on the buccaneers in the room and told them where their customary piracy went wrong. Their purpose had been to create monopolies, raise prices, and pay themselves fat dividends out of privilege. Schwab condemned it in his heartiest manner — as **short-sighted**, because it restricted the market in an era when everything cried for expansion. Cheapen the cost of steel, he argued, and an ever-expanding market is created; more uses for steel get devised; a goodly portion of world trade can be captured.

Without knowing it, Lowell writes, **Schwab was an apostle of modern mass production**.

**The point Hill draws out:** Schwab asked for nothing and pledged nothing. He gave the program away, in full, to a room under no obligation to him — and told them their business model was wrong while he was at it.

## Why it landed when others hadn't

Morgan had been pitched a steel trust before — by Gates, by the Moores, by Gary. The consolidation wave had already swept biscuit, wire and hoop, sugar, rubber, whiskey, oil, and chewing gum, and it was operative in steel: Gates had American Steel and Wire; Morgan had Federal Steel, National Tube, and American Bridge; the Moores had four companies of their own.

Beside Carnegie's vertical trust, all of it was **picayune**. They could combine to their hearts' content and never dent the Carnegie organization, and Morgan knew it. So did Carnegie, who had watched the attempts from Skibo first with amusement and then with resentment — and had resolved, with Schwab as his chief lieutenant, to duplicate every mill his rivals owned and drive them to the wall.

A trust without Carnegie, as one writer put it, was **a plum pudding without the plums**.

What Schwab's speech carried was the inference — not the pledge — that the vast Carnegie enterprise could be brought under the Morgan tent. That was the answer to the problem Morgan could not solve.

## The night at the library

Some economic historians have argued that the whole drama was staged by Carnegie: the dinner, the speech, the Sunday-night conference, all arranged by the canny Scot. **Lowell says the truth is exactly the opposite** — when Schwab was called in, he did not know whether the Little Boss would so much as listen to an offer, least of all from men Andrew regarded as endowed with something less than holiness.

Four men worked over the figures until dawn. Morgan. Bacon. Gates. And Schwab, who knew more about making and selling steel than any whole group of men then living.

Two things about that night carry the weight of the story:

**His figures were never questioned.** If Schwab said a company was worth so much, then it was worth that much and no more. He had arrived with the work already done — six sheets of copperplate, the physical worth and potential earning capacity of every concern that mattered.

**He controlled what went in.** He insisted on including only the companies he nominated: a corporation with no duplication, not even to satisfy the greed of friends wanting to unload their holdings onto the broad Morgan shoulders. He left out by design a number of larger concerns on which "the walruses and carpenters of Wall Street had cast hungry eyes."

At dawn Morgan straightened his back. One question remained.

> *"Do you think you can persuade Andrew Carnegie to sell?"*
> *"I can try."*
> *"If you can get him to sell, I will undertake the matter."*

Schwab's estimate of Carnegie's price: **about $320 million**. Payment in bonds — nobody could raise a third of a billion dollars in cash.

## The golf game and the slip of paper

January, on the frost-cracking heath of the St. Andrews links in Westchester. Carnegie bundled up in sweaters against the cold; Schwab talking volubly, as usual, to keep his spirits up. Not one word of business was mentioned on the course.

Only when the two sat down in the warmth of the Carnegie cottage nearby did Schwab turn it on — the same persuasiveness that had hypnotized eighty millionaires at the University Club, now spent on the glittering promise of retirement and comfort, and of untold millions to satisfy an old man's social caprices.

Carnegie capitulated. He wrote a figure on a slip of paper, handed it over, and said: *all right, that's what we'll sell for.*

In Lowell's account the figure was **approximately $400 million** — Schwab's $320 million base, plus $80 million to represent the increase in capital value over the previous two years. (Most historical accounts give $480 million on the slip; see the divergence section.)

Later, on the deck of a transatlantic liner, the Scotsman said ruefully to Morgan that he wished he had asked for $100 million more.

> *"If you had asked for it, you'd have gotten it,"* Morgan told him cheerfully.

## The uproar, and the absorption

A British correspondent cabled that the foreign steel world was appalled by the gigantic combination. President Hadley of Yale declared that unless trusts were regulated, the country might expect an emperor in Washington inside twenty-five years.

Meanwhile Keene went at the work of shoving the new stock at the public so vigorously that **all the excess water — estimated by some at nearly $600 million — was absorbed in a twinkling.**

Carnegie had his millions. The Morgan syndicate had about $62 million for its trouble. And all the boys, from Gates to Gary, had theirs. Schwab, at 38, was made president of the new corporation.

## The numbers

| | |
|---|---|
| Schwab's estimate of Carnegie's price | ~$320 million (bonds — cash was impossible at that scale) |
| Carnegie's slip of paper | ~$400 million in Lowell's telling; ~$480 million in most histories |
| Final consideration for the Carnegie interests | ~$492 million; Carnegie's personal share ~$226 million |
| Authorized capitalization | ~$1.4 billion — the first billion-dollar corporation in history |
| Rough structure | ~$508M common, ~$500M+ preferred, ~$300–390M bonds (sources differ) |
| Appraised value of the underlying properties | ~$682 million (Bureau of Corporations) |
| The gap — the "water" | ~$600 million |
| Morgan syndicate's take | ~$62 million (Lowell); modern accounts break it into roughly $40M in fees plus a $10M management fee |
| Share of U.S. steel output at formation | roughly two-thirds |

That ~$600 million gap was denounced at the time as **watered stock** — better than half the capitalization with no tangible asset behind it.

Hill's answer is the reason the story lives in a chapter called Faith. The properties rose in value by roughly $600 million **through the mere transaction that consolidated them under one management**. So: Schwab's idea, plus the faith with which he conveyed it to the minds of Morgan and the others, was marketed for a profit of about $600 million. Not an insignificant sum for a single idea.

And Hill closes the loop on whether that was real money or a conjuring trick: U.S. Steel prospered, became one of the richest and most powerful corporations in America, employed thousands, developed new uses for steel and opened new markets — **which is what makes the $600 million earned rather than extracted.**

> Riches begin in the form of thought. The amount is limited only by the person in whose mind the thought is put into motion. Faith removes limitations.

## Where the telling and the record diverge

Worth holding lightly if this gets taught or quoted:

- **Carnegie's price.** Lowell says ~$400 million, and shows his arithmetic ($320M + $80M). Most historical accounts give $480 million written on the slip and ~$492 million as the concluded consideration. This is a real conflict, not a rounding difference.
- **Speech length.** Ninety minutes in Lowell; contemporary press accounts put it nearer a half hour.
- **Who arranged the dinner.** Lowell credits Simmons and Smith and explicitly rejects the theory that Carnegie staged the evening. Several modern accounts say Carnegie helped organize it, attended the reception, and left before the speech. The two versions are not reconcilable, and the disagreement is itself old.
- **"Remained in control until 1930."** Lowell's line, and it is wrong. Schwab's presidency of U.S. Steel ended in 1903, worn down by conflict with Gary, Frick, and the board; William Ellis Corey succeeded him. The 1930 date appears to fold in Schwab's long subsequent reign at Bethlehem Steel. If you quote the article, don't quote this part.
- **The cigar.** Morgan letting his cigar die is universally repeated elsewhere and impossible to verify. It is not in Lowell's account.

One thing Lowell does *not* do is pretend Schwab invented consolidation out of nothing — the article names Gates, the Moores, and Gary as having urged a steel trust first, and describes the merger wave already running through a dozen industries. Schwab's contribution was the blueprint and the credibility, in a room where the one indispensable company was finally in reach. The story is stronger told that way, not weaker.

## Afterward

- Schwab lasted as president only into 1903. William Ellis Corey succeeded him.
- He then took control of Bethlehem Steel and built it into the second largest producer in the country. His second act was arguably his better one — and it, not U.S. Steel, is what he was still running near 1930.
- Carnegie, at 65 and the richest man in the world, turned to giving the money away — libraries, universities, endowments — for the rest of his life.
- U.S. Steel was the largest corporation in the world for decades, and never again held the market share it held the day it opened.
- Hadley of Yale was not entirely wrong to worry. The government did sue to dissolve the trust; the suit is where Schwab's own account of the speech survives at all.

## For the faith angle

The chapter is not really about steel. Hill's argument is that faith is an *applied* faculty — a thing believed, held with definiteness, spoken, and acted on before any evidence arrives that it will work. He is explicit that he expects the objection: *you cannot convert nothing into something.* U.S. Steel is his answer, because the mills and the mechanical equipment came **after** the corporation existed in law, and the corporation existed in law because it had first existed in one man's mind.

The connections that preach:

- **The word goes out before the provision comes in.** Nothing on December 12 required Schwab to speak; he was advised, twice, to say less. He spoke anyway, and the means came looking for him.
- **Faith without figures is enthusiasm.** The six sheets of copperplate are the discipline half — and note *why* they mattered: because his figures were never questioned. Credibility was built long before the night it got spent.
- **He kept the thing clean when he could have loaded it.** He refused to pad the combination with his friends' companies. The most quietly impressive moment in the story is a man with the pen in his hand declining to enrich the people leaning on him.
- **He told them the truth about themselves.** The speech named the error — profit by restriction rather than by abundance — to the men making it.
- **Give before you try to get** is Hill's phrasing of a much older idea, and he knows it.
- **"Practically unknown... merely Andrew Carnegie's man Friday."** Hill's closing emphasis is that the man had no standing until he gave the idea away. Position followed the offering, not the reverse.

One honest caution for teaching: Hill measures faith by what it accumulates, and ends on *both poverty and riches are the offspring of thought.* That is a narrower thing than the faith the ministry means, and it cuts badly if applied to people whose poverty was not authored by their thinking. The story lands better when the difference is said out loud than when it is blurred.
